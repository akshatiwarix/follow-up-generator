# Follow-Up Generator — how it works, in plain English

No code in this one. If you've ever wondered whether an "AI writes your
follow-up email" tool actually respects what was said on the call, or just
writes something plausible-sounding and hopes, this is written for you.

## The problem, in one paragraph

After a sales call, someone owes the prospect a follow-up email and owes
their own pipeline an honest next step. Written from memory hours later,
that follow-up tends to smooth over gaps — "I'll just say we'll be in
touch" — or worse, state something as settled that was never actually
agreed. Ask an AI model to write the same follow-up from a set of notes,
and you get the same failure mode, faster and more confidently: a model
asked to sound helpful will happily invent a next-meeting date, a resolved
objection, or a named stakeholder that was never in the notes, and it does
it with total fluency, no hedge, nothing that looks like a guess. This tool
is a real, live language model writing that follow-up — and a separate,
deterministic check that catches it every time it invents a specific
instead of admitting a gap.

## 1. An AI wrote this — and something else is grading it

Every follow-up draft and next-step list in this tool was generated by a
real model call to Google's Gemini, once per meeting. That's the whole
point of the exercise: this isn't a template with blanks filled in, it's
actual generative writing. But generative writing from an AI is exactly
the thing that's hardest to trust, so the model isn't graded on vibes.

Every sentence the model writes that claims a specific fact — a name, a
date, a commitment, a pain point — comes back with a citation: which field
of the meeting notes it's supposedly quoting, and what value it read there.
A second, completely separate, non-AI piece of code then checks each
citation against the real meeting data. If the model says "cites
nextMeeting.timeframe: next Tuesday" but the actual data has no confirmed
timeframe, that's caught and flagged, automatically, every time — the
model doesn't get to grade its own homework.

## 2. Stating a gap beats inventing a fact

Some of the demo's meetings are deliberately incomplete on purpose: no
confirmed next-meeting date, a commitment that was left vague ("we'll look
into it"), an unresolved objection, a stakeholder who was never named, deal
stage signals that flatly contradict each other. A follow-up email that
papers over these gaps with a confident, specific-sounding sentence is
worse than one that plainly says "we didn't land on a firm date — let me
know your availability."

Across the demo's 8 deliberately-incomplete meetings, the model did this
correctly every single time: zero fabricated specifics, checked
automatically, not eyeballed. One example, verbatim from the committed
demo: for a meeting where no next date was ever confirmed, the generated
draft says *"We did not confirm a timeframe for our next meeting, so please
let me know your availability"* — instead of guessing one.

## 3. "It's grounded" should be a number, not a vibe

Every meeting in the library shows a **groundedness score**, 0 to 100:
roughly, what fraction of the draft's factual claims came with a citation,
multiplied by what fraction of those citations actually checked out against
the real data. It's not stored as a trust-me number either — click into any
meeting and the page recomputes the score live from the committed draft and
context, the same calculation the corpus-build script ran.

Across the 20 meetings in this demo, that averages to **82 out of 100** —
and, same as any real measurement, it's not identical across the easy and
hard cases: clean meetings average **83.3**, the deliberately incomplete
ones average **79.9**. That gap running the expected direction is the
point — a script that scored identically on easy and hard meetings would
mean the "difficulty" label wasn't actually connected to anything real.

## 4. You can make the model do this live, right now

The library's 20 meetings were generated once and committed, so loading the
page costs nothing and never waits on a model call. But there's a second
page — **Try It Yourself** — where you fill in your own small, fictional
meeting (a company, a contact, a commitment, whether the next meeting was
actually confirmed) and a real model call generates a follow-up for it,
live, with the same citation-and-groundedness treatment. Leave the next
meeting unconfirmed and watch whether it invents a date anyway.

## What this tool doesn't try to do

It only produces one follow-up per meeting — there's no multi-meeting
account rollup, and no write-back into an actual CRM; the output is a draft
a rep would read, edit, and send themselves. The Try It Yourself page has
no accuracy grade beyond groundedness, because there's no pre-written
ground truth for a meeting you just typed in — the tool doesn't pretend to
grade what it has no answer key for. And it isn't a general-purpose email
assistant: the input is a small structured form, not a paragraph of loose
notes, because grounding a citation to an exact field only works if the
model and the grader agree on the same shape of data.
